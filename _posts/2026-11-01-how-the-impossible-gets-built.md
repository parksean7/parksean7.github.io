In the early twentieth century, New York City was among the wealthiest and most dynamic cities in the world. Yet it repeatedly struggled to build the infrastructure its growing population needed. Traffic congestion was worsening, public spaces were increasingly scarce, and the need for new bridges, roads, and parks was obvious.

**The problem wasn't knowing what needed to be built. It was figuring out how to get it built.**

Building even a single bridge required enormous capital, government approvals, and land from often unwilling owners. Each obstacle was difficult enough on its own. But the real challenge was how deeply they were intertwined.

Investors needed confidence in future revenues, yet those revenues would not exist until the infrastructure was built. Government approval meant little if landowners refused to cooperate. Decisions that made perfect sense to individual stakeholders could collectively prevent projects that would benefit society as a whole.

The greatest barriers to ambitious projects, in other words, were not always technological. Financial, legal, political, and organizational constraints could reinforce one another until a project became all but impossible to execute.

Robert Moses never won a single election, yet he built more than 1,000 kilometers of roads and numerous bridges across the New York metropolitan area, effectively redrawing the map of modern New York.

What set Moses apart was not his engineering expertise, but his ability to overcome the financial, legal, and political obstacles that had stalled ambitious projects for decades. He found ways to turn plans that had languished on drawing boards into bridges, highways, and parks.

Decades later, Elon Musk confronted a different version of the same underlying problem.

Humanity had been launching rockets into space for decades, and electric vehicles were hardly a new invention. Yet rockets remained extraordinarily expensive to launch, while high battery costs and limited production capacity kept electric cars from reaching the mass market.

**The challenge was no longer proving that these technologies worked. It was making them economically viable at scale.**

SpaceX pursued reusable rockets to change the economics of spaceflight. Tesla worked to lower battery and manufacturing costs while scaling production. And with Starlink, SpaceX began building a business that could generate demand for its own launch services.

Moses and Musk faced fundamentally different obstacles. Moses had to navigate fragmented political authority and competing interests. Musk confronted technological uncertainty, high production costs, and the challenges of scaling complex systems.

**Rather than accepting the constraints that made their projects seem impossible, they sought to redesign those constraints themselves.**

Three principles emerge from their stories:

1. **What makes a project seem impossible may not be the technology, but the economics.**
2. **Reducing external dependencies is essential to getting things done.**
3. **Build systems in which each success makes the next one easier.**

## 1. What Makes a Project Seem Impossible May Not Be the Technology, but the Economics

When Robert Moses took control of the Triborough Bridge Authority in 1934, construction had stalled amid the Great Depression. The need for a direct connection between Manhattan, Queens, and the Bronx was clear, and the engineering was within reach. What New York lacked was the money to finish it.

Rather than relying entirely on the city's limited borrowing capacity, Moses combined federal construction funding with bonds backed by future toll revenues. The bridge would generate income once completed, and investors could lend against that future revenue to help finance its construction. The Triborough Bridge opened in 1936.

But Moses saw something more consequential than a completed bridge.

**A bridge was not merely infrastructure. It was a revenue-generating asset that could help finance the next piece of infrastructure.**

Financing the bridge was one challenge. Building a financial institution that could fund future projects was another.

Under the conventional arrangement, a public authority would issue bonds to build a bridge, collect tolls until the debt was repaid, then transfer the bridge to the city and dissolve. Through carefully drafted legislation, however, the Triborough Authority gained the power to refinance its debt and undertake additional projects. Rather than disappearing after its original work was paid for, it could continue borrowing against its revenues and expanding its operations.

By 1946, New York City had only about $150 million in borrowing capacity left before reaching its legal debt limit. The Triborough Authority, by contrast, was estimated to have $360 million in borrowing capacity—more than twice the city's remaining headroom.

**An agency created to build bridges had acquired greater borrowing flexibility than the government of America's largest city.**

Moses had helped create something more enduring than a single bridge: a financial engine capable of supporting an expanding network of infrastructure.

Decades later, Elon Musk confronted a different economic barrier.

By the time SpaceX was founded in 2002, humanity had been launching rockets into orbit for decades. Yet access to space remained extraordinarily expensive, partly because rockets were largely disposable.

A rocket's first stage contains sophisticated engines, fuel tanks, and expensive flight hardware. Traditionally, much of that investment was discarded after a single launch.

**What if the most expensive part of a rocket didn't have to be built again every time it flew?**

SpaceX set out to recover the first stage, land it vertically, and fly it again. The engineering challenge was formidable: the booster had to survive separation, atmospheric reentry, and a precisely controlled landing. But landing was only half the problem. Reuse would make economic sense only if refurbishment cost substantially less than building a replacement.

After years of testing and failed landing attempts, SpaceX successfully landed a Falcon 9 first stage in 2015 and flew a previously used booster again in 2017.

Musk estimated that the first stage accounted for roughly 70% of the cost of a new Falcon 9 rocket. By 2020, he was citing refurbishment costs of around $1 million per booster and a best-case marginal launch cost of approximately $15 million, excluding overhead. While these were Musk's estimates rather than audited figures, they pointed to the economic advantage of reusing expensive hardware instead of rebuilding it for every mission.

As boosters began flying repeatedly, reuse became part of SpaceX's regular launch operations.

**SpaceX had turned what was once a recurring expense into a reusable asset.**

**One turned future revenues into present financing. The other turned expendable hardware into reusable capital.**

Rather than treating economic constraints as fixed limits, they treated the economics themselves as something that could be redesigned.

## 2. Reducing External Dependencies Is Essential to Getting Things Done

Making a project economically viable is one thing. Getting it built is another. Even with sufficient funding, progress can stall when critical decisions lie in someone else's hands.

Robert Moses learned this lesson the hard way.

Early in his career, Moses devoted himself to reforming New York's civil service system. His carefully designed proposals threatened the political patronage networks that controlled government appointments—and were ultimately defeated. The experience taught him that even the best-designed reforms meant little without the power to implement them.

Moses eventually found a way to overcome this problem through public authorities.

In 1934, he became New York City's Parks Commissioner while also taking control of the Triborough Bridge Authority. Over time, he accumulated leadership positions across multiple agencies responsible for parks, highways, bridges, and public construction. Decisions that once required negotiations between separate agencies could increasingly be coordinated within the offices Moses himself controlled.

He applied the same instinct for integration to the organization of work itself.

When Moses took charge of New York City's parks in 1934, tens of thousands of federally funded relief workers were already available. Yet many lacked proper tools, supervision, and clear assignments. Moses brought in experienced administrators and engineers, reorganized the work, and completed approximately 1,700 park renovation projects within months.

**Moses did not simply acquire more resources. He gained the ability to put existing resources to work.**

Decades later, Elon Musk encountered a different version of the same problem.

When Musk first explored entering the space industry, he considered buying existing rockets. But the prices led him to question the industry's cost structure. In a 2012 interview with *WIRED*, he recalled calculating that the raw materials in a rocket accounted for only about 2% of its selling price.

The remaining cost was not simply waste: rockets require sophisticated engineering, manufacturing, and testing. But the calculation raised a more interesting question: how much of the price reflected unavoidable technical complexity, and how much reflected the way the industry was organized?

Traditional aerospace manufacturing relied on layers of specialized contractors and subcontractors. Each added its own costs, schedules, and contractual requirements. A design change could ripple through multiple companies, turning an engineering decision into a lengthy process of negotiation and coordination.

SpaceX took a different approach. By bringing much of the design and manufacturing of rocket engines, structures, avionics, and flight software in-house, it could more closely integrate engineering, production, and testing.

**When the people designing a component, manufacturing it, and testing it work within the same organization, the feedback loop becomes much shorter.** Engineers can identify a problem, revise the design, manufacture a replacement, and test it without waiting for an entire chain of external contractors to respond.

The extent of SpaceX's integration is striking. According to a 2026 estimate by RBC Capital Markets, SpaceX manufactures roughly 90% of its hardware in-house, compared with approximately 40% at Boeing, although the companies' product mixes and the precise basis of comparison differ.

But Musk applied the same underlying logic beyond SpaceX.

At Tesla, scaling electric vehicle production required a battery supply chain capable of growing just as quickly. Rather than waiting for the existing battery industry to expand on its own, Tesla partnered with Panasonic to build the Gigafactory, bringing cell production and battery-pack manufacturing into a closely coordinated operation.

Tesla confronted a different dependency with charging infrastructure. Consumers were reluctant to buy electric cars without reliable charging networks, while charging providers had little incentive to invest before enough electric cars were on the road. Tesla responded by building its own Supercharger network.

**The Gigafactory addressed a constraint on how many cars Tesla could produce. The Supercharger network addressed a constraint on how many it could sell.**

Across these businesses, the strategy was not to manufacture everything or eliminate every outside partner. Tesla still relied on Panasonic, just as SpaceX continued to work with specialized suppliers. The goal was more selective: identify the external dependencies that constrained the entire project, then bring those capabilities under closer control.

Moses consolidated political and administrative authority. Musk integrated engineering, manufacturing, and critical infrastructure. **One reorganized the machinery of government; the other reconfigured the boundaries of the firm.**

The real advantage of integration is not simply greater control. It is reducing the delays and dependencies that arise when critical decisions are made elsewhere.

## 3. Build Systems in Which Each Success Makes the Next One Easier

Completing a difficult project is an achievement. Building a system that makes the next one easier is something more consequential.

The Triborough Bridge did more than connect three boroughs. It generated a steady stream of toll revenue, giving Robert Moses the financial means to undertake further construction. But money alone does not explain how he became one of the most powerful figures in New York.

**Moses discovered that the money generated by public works could be used not only to finance more construction, but to build political support for it.**

Every major infrastructure project created economic opportunities. Banks earned fees from underwriting bonds and managing accounts. Construction companies received contracts. Labor unions gained jobs. Lawyers and insurance brokers collected fees and commissions.

Moses controlled access to many of these opportunities.

Robert Caro documents how deliberately he used that control. At one point, Triborough was distributing roughly $1 million annually in public relations retainers, legal fees, and insurance commissions, much of it directed toward politically connected individuals.

In 1948, Triborough deposited approximately $15 million in non-interest-bearing accounts at Federation Bank, an institution connected to political power broker Tom Shanahan. According to Caro, the bank held roughly $35 million in deposits at the time. The arrangement provided a substantial financial benefit to an institution with valuable political connections.

Moses did not need to demand an explicit favor in exchange for every contract or deposit. He cultivated a network in which supporting his projects and benefiting from his spending went hand in hand.

Banks had reasons to preserve their relationships with him. Contractors with expensive equipment and large workforces needed a continuing pipeline of projects. Political leaders benefited from jobs, business opportunities, and visible improvements in their districts.

**The more Moses built, the more people had a financial stake in seeing him build again.**

And when opposition arose, that network could be mobilized.

During a dispute over federal funding for Lincoln Center, Moses drew on influential bankers, labor leaders, and other prominent figures who pressured officials in Washington. The federal government ultimately accepted a compromise that allowed the project to move forward.

The episode illustrated how far his influence extended. Moses had assembled a network capable of exerting political pressure well beyond the formal authority of the agencies he controlled.

The system reinforced itself. Completed infrastructure generated revenue. Revenue supported new projects and sustained relationships with influential beneficiaries. Those relationships, in turn, helped Moses overcome political obstacles and pursue further construction.

**He had turned the act of building into a means of acquiring the power to build more.**

Decades later, Elon Musk developed a different kind of self-reinforcing system.

Reusable rockets made frequent launches more economically practical. But reusability alone did not guarantee enough customers to sustain a high launch cadence. The more often a booster could fly, the more opportunities SpaceX had to spread its hardware costs across multiple missions.

What if SpaceX could generate its own demand for launches?

Starlink provided a way to do exactly that.

Building a global satellite internet network requires launching thousands of satellites, replacing aging spacecraft, and continually expanding capacity. For a company dependent on outside launch providers, this would mean enormous costs and exposure to someone else's launch schedule.

SpaceX, however, already had its own rockets.

Reusable Falcon 9 boosters enabled repeated satellite deployments, while Starlink gave SpaceX a large and recurring source of internal launch demand.

In 2025, SpaceX conducted 165 Falcon launches. Of those, 122 were internal missions, compared with 43 primarily serving external customers. Nearly three-quarters of its Falcon launches were driven by its own businesses.

**Starlink did more than create demand for SpaceX's rockets. It gave SpaceX the launch frequency needed to make its reusable system increasingly valuable.**

Frequent launches created opportunities to refine booster inspection and refurbishment, improve launch operations, and spread fixed infrastructure costs across more missions. Meanwhile, Starlink's expanding subscriber base generated service revenue that could support further investment in the satellite network and SpaceX's broader operations.

SpaceX had not merely found a cheaper way to launch satellites. **It had built a business that needed those launches—and could help finance more of them.**

Moses and Musk had built fundamentally different systems.

Moses used the economic value created by infrastructure to cultivate the financial and political support needed for further construction. Musk used the capabilities created by one business to make another possible, then used that second business to generate demand and resources for the first.

Yet both discovered how to make the results of one project strengthen the system responsible for building the next.

**The most powerful projects do not simply deliver an outcome. They create the resources, relationships, and capabilities that make the next ambitious undertaking possible.**

## Conclusion: The Architecture of Getting Things Done

What makes an ambitious project impossible? More often than we realize, the answer depends on the assumptions we take for granted: how much something must cost, who must approve it, and which resources are available to build it.

Moses found ways to finance infrastructure that New York could not otherwise afford, consolidated authority that had been scattered across government agencies, and turned completed projects into sources of financial and political influence. Musk challenged the economics of expendable rockets, brought critical engineering and manufacturing capabilities under closer control, and built businesses whose growth reinforced one another.

Neither eliminated the underlying difficulties. Building bridges still required enormous capital and political cooperation. Launching rockets still demanded extraordinary engineering precision. What changed was the structure of the constraints that had made those undertakings so difficult.

First, change the economics. A project that cannot be financed or operated under one cost structure may become viable under another.

Second, reduce external dependencies. Even a viable project can stall when critical decisions and capabilities remain beyond the reach of those responsible for executing it.

Third, make success compound. The most consequential projects create more than their intended results. They generate capital, capabilities, relationships, and demand that can make future projects easier to undertake.

**The first makes a project viable. The second makes it executable. The third makes success repeatable.**

Yet Moses's story also reveals a tension at the heart of this approach. The same institutions that allowed him to overcome bureaucratic paralysis eventually made his power extraordinarily difficult to challenge. By concentrating financial resources and political influence, he gained the ability to build at remarkable speed—but also weakened the mechanisms through which the public could question what he chose to build.

Removing obstacles to execution is not always the same as removing obstacles to progress. Some constraints reflect needless inefficiency. Others protect interests that the people driving a project might otherwise overlook. That distinction matters. **The ability to get things done does not, by itself, establish that they should be done.**

When we look at a bridge, a rocket, or a satellite network, we tend to see the finished achievement. What is less visible is the system that made it possible: the financing arrangements, organizational structures, technical capabilities, and relationships that turned an ambitious idea into reality. And perhaps the most consequential achievement is not the finished project at all, but the capacity it leaves behind.

The real question is not simply how to build the impossible, but how to build a system that makes the impossible possible again and again.
