In the early twentieth century, New York City faced a curious paradox.

One of the wealthiest and most dynamic cities in the world, New York repeatedly struggled to build the public infrastructure its people desperately needed. Its population was booming, traffic congestion was worsening, and public spaces were increasingly scarce. The need for new bridges, roads, and parks was obvious to everyone.

The problem wasn't knowing what needed to be built. It was figuring out how to get it built.

Building even a single bridge required enormous capital. Securing the money was only the beginning: government approvals had to be obtained, land had to be acquired, and competing interests had to be reconciled. Property owners resisted giving up their land. Politicians feared backlash from their constituents. Government agencies operated under different mandates and budgets, and a setback at any stage could bring the entire project to a halt.

What made these obstacles especially formidable was how deeply intertwined they were.

Financing depended on demonstrating a project's economic viability, yet proving that viability often required building the infrastructure first. Acquiring land required political approval, but approval alone did little to resolve opposition from those whose interests were at stake.

Decisions that made perfect sense to each individual stakeholder could collectively prevent projects that would benefit society as a whole.

The greatest barriers to ambitious projects, in other words, were not always technological. Financial, legal, political, and organizational constraints could reinforce one another until a project became all but impossible to execute.

Few people understood how to break through these constraints better than Robert Moses.

Robert Moses. A man who never won a single election, yet built more than 1,000 kilometers of roads and numerous bridges across the New York metropolitan area, effectively redrawing the map of modern New York.

What set Moses apart was not his mastery of engineering, but his ability to solve problems that had frustrated others for decades. He turned future toll revenues into immediate construction financing, consolidated authority scattered across government agencies, and used the resources and influence gained from one project to make the next possible.

Projects that had languished on drawing boards for decades finally took shape as bridges, highways, and parks.

Decades later, in entirely different industries, another figure began confronting remarkably similar challenges.

Elon Musk.

Humanity had been launching rockets into space for decades. Electric vehicles were hardly a new invention. Yet launching rockets remained extraordinarily expensive, while high battery costs and limited production capacity kept electric cars from reaching the mass market.

The question was no longer simply whether something could be built, but whether it could be built economically, at scale, and over and over again.

SpaceX challenged the economics of expendable rockets by developing reusable launch systems. Tesla focused on bringing down battery and manufacturing costs while scaling up production. SpaceX went a step further by pairing reusable rockets with Starlink, creating a system in which the growth of one business reinforced the competitiveness of the other.

Of course, the obstacles Moses and Musk faced were fundamentally different. Moses had to navigate fragmented political authority and competing interests. Musk confronted greater technological uncertainty, high production costs, and the challenges of scaling complex systems.

Yet beneath these differences lies a striking similarity in their approach.

Rather than simply throwing more resources at problems within existing constraints, they sought to change the underlying structures that made their projects so difficult in the first place.

Three principles emerge from their stories:

1. What makes a project seem impossible may not be the technology, but the economics.
2. Reducing external dependencies is essential to getting things done.
3. Build systems in which each success makes the next one easier.

Together, these principles offer a way to understand how seemingly impossible projects become reality—and how one breakthrough can lay the foundation for the next.

## 1. What Makes a Project Seem Impossible May Not Be the Technology, but the Economics.

A project can be technically feasible and still be economically impossible.

Knowing how to build something is not the same as being able to afford it. Sometimes, the real breakthrough comes not from inventing new technology, but from changing how a project is financed—or what it costs to operate.

Robert Moses understood this distinction remarkably well.

When Moses took control of the Triborough Bridge Authority in 1934, construction had stalled amid the Great Depression. The engineering was feasible. The need for a direct connection between Manhattan, Queens, and the Bronx was clear. But New York City, already struggling with its finances, could not readily afford to finish the bridge.

Moses found another way.

Rather than relying entirely on the city's limited borrowing capacity, he combined federal construction funding with bonds backed by future toll revenues. The bridge would generate income once completed, and investors could lend against that future revenue to help finance its construction.

The Triborough Bridge opened in 1936.

But Moses saw something more consequential than a completed bridge.

A bridge was not merely infrastructure. It was a revenue-generating asset that could help finance the next piece of infrastructure.

Traditionally, a public authority would issue bonds to build a bridge, collect tolls until the debt was repaid, then transfer the bridge to the city and dissolve.

Moses helped transform that arrangement.

Through carefully drafted legislation, the Triborough Authority gained the power to refinance its debt and undertake additional projects. Instead of disappearing once its original bridges were paid for, it could continue borrowing against its revenues and expanding its operations.

The scale of this transformation was extraordinary.

In 1946, New York City had only about $150 million in borrowing capacity left before reaching its legal debt limit. The Triborough Authority, by contrast, was estimated to have $360 million in borrowing capacity—more than twice the city's remaining headroom.

An agency created to build bridges had acquired greater borrowing flexibility than the government of America's largest city.

Moses had not simply found the money to complete one project. He had helped create a financial engine capable of funding an expanding network of infrastructure.

Decades later, Elon Musk confronted a different economic barrier.

By the time SpaceX was founded in 2002, humanity had been launching rockets into orbit for decades. The technology existed. But access to space remained extraordinarily expensive.

The problem was partly built into the industry's economics: rockets were largely disposable.

Consider the numbers. A 2018 NASA technical report estimated the Space Shuttle's launch cost at roughly $54,500 per kilogram of payload capacity, compared with Falcon 9's advertised price of approximately $2,720 per kilogram—a twentyfold difference.

The comparison was not perfectly like-for-like, and SpaceX had already made major cost reductions before achieving reusability. But it illustrated just how dramatically the economics of reaching orbit could change.

Musk and SpaceX wanted to push those economics further.

A rocket's first stage contains sophisticated engines, fuel tanks, and expensive flight hardware. Traditionally, much of that investment was discarded after a single launch.

What if the most expensive part of a rocket didn't have to be built again every time it flew?

SpaceX set out to recover the first stage, land it vertically, and fly it again.

The engineering challenge was formidable. The booster had to survive separation, atmospheric reentry, and a precisely controlled landing. But landing was only half the problem. Reuse would make economic sense only if refurbishment cost substantially less than building a replacement.

After years of testing and failed landing attempts, SpaceX successfully landed a Falcon 9 first stage in 2015 and flew a previously used booster again in 2017.

The economics began to change.

Musk estimated that the first stage accounted for roughly 70% of a Falcon 9's launch cost. By 2020, he was citing refurbishment costs of around $1 million per booster and a best-case marginal launch cost of approximately $15 million, excluding overhead.

Instead of repeatedly manufacturing its most expensive component, SpaceX could recover it, inspect it, refurbish it, and launch it again.

Reusability eventually became routine rather than experimental.

SpaceX had turned what was once a recurring expense into a reusable asset.

Moses and Musk had confronted different economic constraints.

Moses needed to finance enormous upfront construction costs. Musk needed to reduce the recurring cost of launching rockets.

One turned future revenues into present financing. The other turned expendable hardware into reusable capital.

Yet the underlying principle was the same.

Rather than treating economic constraints as fixed limits, they treated the economics themselves as something that could be redesigned.

But changing the economics is only part of the challenge.

Even a financially viable project can remain stalled when critical decisions, resources, and capabilities are scattered across organizations with competing priorities.

That brings us to the second principle:

## 2. Reducing External Dependencies Is Essential to Getting Things Done.

Making a project economically viable is one thing. Getting it built is another.

Even with sufficient funding, a project can grind to a halt when critical decisions lie in someone else's hands. A bridge may depend on approvals from multiple government agencies. A rocket may depend on suppliers whose costs and schedules are beyond its manufacturer's control.

The problem is not always a lack of resources. Sometimes, it is a lack of control over the decisions that determine how those resources are used.

Robert Moses learned this lesson the hard way.

Early in his career, Moses spent years advocating civil service reform in New York. His proposals were meticulously researched, but they threatened the political patronage system that powerful interests depended on. Despite his efforts, the reforms were defeated.

The experience taught him that having the right ideas was not enough. Without the authority to implement them, even the most carefully designed plans could go nowhere.

Moses eventually found a way to overcome this problem through public authorities.

In 1934, he became New York City's Parks Commissioner while also taking control of the Triborough Bridge Authority. Over time, he accumulated leadership positions across multiple agencies responsible for parks, highways, bridges, and public construction.

These positions gave him something far more valuable than impressive titles: the ability to coordinate decisions that had previously been scattered across different branches of government.

Instead of repeatedly negotiating with separate agencies over funding, land acquisition, construction, and operations, Moses could bring many of those decisions under his own direction.

The difference was immediate.

When Moses took charge of New York City's parks in 1934, tens of thousands of federally funded relief workers were already available. Yet much of that labor was being wasted. Crews lacked proper tools, materials, supervision, and clear instructions.

Moses brought in experienced engineers and administrators, reorganized the work, and put the existing workforce to productive use.

Within roughly four months, approximately 1,700 park renovation projects had been completed.

The workers and much of the funding had already been there. What changed was the organization responsible for turning those resources into results.

Moses did not simply acquire more resources. He gained the ability to put existing resources to work.

Decades later, Elon Musk encountered a different version of the same problem.

When Musk first explored entering the space industry, he considered purchasing existing rockets rather than building them from scratch. But the prices he encountered were far higher than he was willing to accept.

He began questioning the economics of the aerospace supply chain itself.

In a 2012 interview with WIRED, Musk described calculating the market value of the raw materials in a rocket—aluminum, titanium, copper, and carbon fiber—and finding that they represented only about 2% of its selling price.

That did not mean the remaining 98% was waste. Rockets require sophisticated engineering, precision manufacturing, extensive testing, and highly specialized labor.

But it raised a fundamental question: How much of the cost was truly unavoidable, and how much came from the way the industry was organized?

Traditional aerospace manufacturing relied on layers of specialized contractors and subcontractors. Each added its own costs, schedules, and contractual requirements. A design change could ripple through multiple companies, turning an engineering decision into a lengthy process of negotiation and coordination.

SpaceX took a different approach.

Rather than outsourcing the development of its most critical systems, it brought much of the design and manufacturing of rocket engines, structures, avionics, and flight software in-house.

The contrast with traditional aerospace manufacturing is striking. According to a 2026 estimate by RBC Capital Markets, SpaceX manufactures roughly 90% of its hardware in-house, compared with approximately 40% at Boeing.

The significance goes beyond avoiding supplier markups.

When the people designing a component, manufacturing it, and testing it work within the same organization, the feedback loop becomes much shorter.

Engineers can identify a problem, revise the design, manufacture a replacement, and test it without waiting for an entire chain of external contractors to respond.

This was especially valuable in rocket development, where progress depends on repeated testing and rapid learning from failure.

And Musk applied the same underlying logic beyond SpaceX.

At Tesla, the challenge was not simply designing a compelling electric car. Scaling production required a battery supply chain capable of growing just as quickly.

Rather than waiting for the existing battery industry to expand on its own, Tesla partnered with Panasonic to build the Gigafactory, bringing cell production and battery-pack manufacturing into a closely coordinated operation.

Tesla confronted a similar problem with charging infrastructure.

Even a capable electric car would struggle to attract buyers if drivers could not reliably charge it on long journeys. Yet charging providers had limited incentives to build a nationwide network before enough electric cars were on the road.

Tesla responded by building its own Supercharger network, reducing its dependence on an infrastructure market that had not yet developed.

Across these businesses, the strategy was not to manufacture everything or eliminate every outside partner. Tesla still relied on Panasonic, just as SpaceX continued to work with specialized suppliers.

The goal was more selective: identify the external dependencies that constrained the entire project, then bring those capabilities under closer control.

Moses and Musk operated in fundamentally different environments.

Moses consolidated political and administrative authority. Musk integrated engineering, manufacturing, and critical infrastructure. One reorganized the machinery of government; the other reconfigured the boundaries of the firm.

Yet both confronted the same underlying problem: their ability to execute depended on decisions made by others.

And both sought to change that.

The real advantage of integration is not simply having more control. It is removing the bottlenecks created when the next critical step depends on someone else's priorities.

But there is a further implication.

Once an organization can finance its projects and coordinate their execution, the capabilities it builds do not disappear when the first project is completed.

They can become the foundation for the next.

That leads to the third principle:

## 3. Build Systems in Which Each Success Makes the Next One Easier.

Completing a difficult project is an achievement. Building a system that makes the next one easier is something more consequential.

Most projects consume resources to produce a finished result. But some create resources of their own—capital, capabilities, demand, and influence that can be used to pursue the next project.

Robert Moses understood how powerful this could become.

The Triborough Bridge did more than connect three boroughs. It generated a steady stream of toll revenue, giving Moses the financial means to undertake further construction.

But money alone does not explain how he became one of the most powerful figures in New York.

Moses discovered that the money generated by public works could be used not only to finance more construction, but to build political support for it.

Every major infrastructure project created economic opportunities. Banks could earn fees from underwriting bonds and managing accounts. Construction companies received contracts. Labor unions gained jobs. Lawyers and insurance brokers collected fees and commissions.

Moses controlled access to many of these opportunities.

And he understood that the people who benefited from his projects were often the very people whose political influence could help him secure approval for the next.

Robert Caro documents the scale of this arrangement in *The Power Broker*. At one point, Triborough was distributing roughly $1 million a year in public relations retainers, legal fees, and insurance commissions.

These were not merely administrative expenses. According to Caro, Moses directed lucrative business toward politically connected individuals whose influence could protect his authority and advance his projects.

The mechanism was subtle but powerful.

Moses did not necessarily demand an explicit favor in exchange for every contract. Instead, he cultivated a network in which supporting his projects and benefiting from his spending went hand in hand.

The relationships reinforced themselves.

Banks that profited from Triborough's financing had reasons to preserve their relationship with Moses. Contractors with expensive equipment and large workforces needed a continuing pipeline of projects. Political leaders benefited from jobs, business opportunities, and visible improvements in their districts.

The more Moses built, the more people had a financial stake in seeing him build again.

And that support mattered when a project encountered opposition.

Influential bankers, contractors, union leaders, and political figures could exert pressure on officials, mobilize support, and help clear the political obstacles that might otherwise have delayed construction.

Moses had created a self-reinforcing system.

Completed infrastructure generated revenue. Revenue financed new projects and sustained a network of influential beneficiaries. Their support helped Moses secure the authority and cooperation needed to build more infrastructure.

Over time, the system became increasingly difficult to challenge—not simply because Moses controlled money, but because so many powerful interests had become invested in his continued success.

He had turned the act of building into a means of acquiring the power to build more.

Decades later, Elon Musk built a different kind of self-reinforcing system.

By developing reusable rockets, SpaceX had made frequent launches more economically practical. But lowering launch costs did not automatically guarantee enough customers to sustain a high launch cadence.

The economics of reusability depend in part on how often the hardware flies. A rocket that can be reused many times delivers more value when there is enough demand to keep launching it.

That created a new question.

What if SpaceX could generate its own demand for launches?

Starlink provided the answer.

Building a global satellite internet network requires launching thousands of satellites, replacing aging spacecraft, and continually expanding capacity. For a company dependent on outside launch providers, that would mean enormous costs and exposure to someone else's launch schedule.

SpaceX, however, already had its own rockets.

Reusable Falcon 9 boosters made it possible to deploy satellites repeatedly, while Starlink gave SpaceX a large and recurring source of internal launch demand.

The scale of this relationship became remarkable.

In 2025, SpaceX conducted 165 Falcon launches. Of those, 122 were internal missions, compared with 43 primarily serving external customers.

Nearly three-quarters of its Falcon launches were therefore driven by its own businesses.

This changed the economics on both sides.

For Starlink, access to SpaceX's launch capacity reduced dependence on external launch providers and supported rapid constellation expansion.

For SpaceX's launch business, Starlink created a substantial base of recurring demand, supporting high flight rates, repeated use of boosters, and the accumulation of operational experience.

And as Starlink expanded its subscriber base, it created a growing source of service revenue that could support further investment across SpaceX.

SpaceX had not merely found a cheaper way to launch satellites. It had built a business that needed those launches—and could help finance more of them.

This was a different mechanism from Moses's political network, but the underlying structure was strikingly similar.

Moses used the economic value created by infrastructure to attract the financial and political support needed for further construction.

Musk used the capabilities created by one business to make another possible, then used that second business to generate demand and resources for the first.

In both cases, the result of one project became an input to the next.

The important distinction is between completing projects independently and building a system in which projects reinforce one another.

The most powerful projects do not simply deliver an outcome. They create the resources, relationships, and capabilities that make the next ambitious undertaking possible.

And that brings the three principles together.

Changing the economics makes an otherwise unviable project possible. Reducing external dependencies gives an organization greater control over execution. Building a self-reinforcing system allows the benefits of one success to carry forward into the next.

The deeper lesson is that extraordinary execution is not always a matter of overcoming the same obstacles through greater effort.

Sometimes, it comes from building a system in which those obstacles become progressively easier to overcome.

## Conclusion: The Architecture of Getting Things Done

What makes an ambitious project impossible?

More often than we realize, the answer depends on the assumptions we take for granted: how much something must cost, who must approve it, and which resources are available to build it.

Robert Moses and Elon Musk challenged those assumptions in different ways.

Moses found ways to finance infrastructure that New York could not otherwise afford, consolidated authority that had been scattered across government agencies, and turned completed projects into sources of financial and political influence.

Musk challenged the economics of expendable rockets, brought critical engineering and manufacturing capabilities under closer control, and built businesses whose growth reinforced one another.

Neither eliminated the underlying difficulties. Building bridges still required enormous capital and political cooperation. Launching rockets still demanded extraordinary engineering precision. What changed was the structure of the constraints that had made those undertakings so difficult.

Their stories reveal three connected principles.

First, change the economics. A project that cannot be financed or operated under one cost structure may become viable under another.

Second, reduce external dependencies. Even a viable project can stall when critical decisions and capabilities remain beyond the reach of those responsible for executing it.

Third, make success compound. The most consequential projects create more than their intended results. They generate capital, capabilities, relationships, and demand that can make future projects easier to undertake.

The first makes a project viable. The second makes it executable. The third makes success repeatable.

Yet Moses's story also reveals a tension at the heart of this approach.

The same institutions that allowed him to overcome bureaucratic paralysis eventually made his power extraordinarily difficult to challenge. By concentrating financial resources and political influence, he gained the ability to build at remarkable speed—but also weakened the mechanisms through which the public could question what he chose to build.

Removing obstacles to execution is not always the same as removing obstacles to progress. Some constraints reflect needless inefficiency. Others protect interests that the people driving a project might otherwise overlook.

That distinction matters. The ability to get things done does not, by itself, establish that they should be done.

Still, the central insight remains.

When we look at a bridge, a rocket, or a satellite network, we tend to see the finished achievement. What is less visible is the system that made it possible: the financing arrangements, organizational structures, technical capabilities, and relationships that turned an ambitious idea into reality.

And perhaps the most consequential achievement is not the finished project at all, but the capacity it leaves behind.

The real question is not simply how to build the impossible, but how to build a system that makes the impossible possible again and again.
